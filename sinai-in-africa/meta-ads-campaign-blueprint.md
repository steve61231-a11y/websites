### 0.3 Claim discipline for this specific product — non-negotiable

The single biggest way this campaign fails is not bad creative. It is **making a claim the book cannot support**, getting the ad rejected or the account restricted, and burning a budget you cannot replace.

| Category | How to phrase it in ads | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Established fact** | State plainly | "Egypt is in Africa." "The Sinai Peninsula links Africa and Asia." |
| **Long-standing tradition (not proof)** | Name it as tradition | "The traditional site has been honoured since the Byzantine era." |
| **Genuine scholarly uncertainty** | State as uncertainty | "There is no scholarly consensus on where Mount Sinai stood." |
| **The author's interpretation** | Attribute it | "The author argues…" / "This book makes the case that…" |
| **Disputed claim** | Attribute + invite scrutiny | "A claim the book examines — and asks you to weigh." |
| **Hypothesis** | Frame as a question | "What if we've been looking in the wrong place?" |
| **Reader's conclusion** | Hand it over | "Read the evidence. Decide for yourself." |

**Facts you can safely lean on** (verified this session):
- Mount Sinai **has never been identified archaeologically with scholarly consensus** — [Biblical Archaeology Society](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-topics/exodus/searching-for-biblical-mt-sinai/), [Bible Interp](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/Anati_Mount_Sinai)
- Jabal Musa's identification as Mt. Sinai **developed in the early Byzantine period** with monasticism — it is a tradition of custody, not an excavation result — [BAS](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-topics/exodus/searching-for-biblical-mt-sinai/)
- **Multiple serious candidate locations compete**: Jabal Musa (south Sinai), Har Karkom (Negev), north-west Arabia / Midian, Hashem el-Tarif — [Wikipedia: Mount Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Sinai), [Mount Karkom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Karkom)
- **No Exodus-related archaeological remains** have been recovered in the Sinai Peninsula for the traditional Exodus period — [BAS](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-topics/exodus/searching-for-biblical-mt-sinai/)
- The **Sinai Peninsula straddles the Africa/Asia boundary**: usually classed geographically as south-west Asia, but frequently grouped with Africa geopolitically — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sinai-Peninsula), [New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Sinai_Peninsula)

That last one is your **strongest honest hook**, and it costs you nothing in credibility: the question "which continent was Mount Sinai on?" is *genuinely* unsettled at the level of basic geography, before anyone even argues about the book's thesis.

## 1.3 Advantage+ Creative enhancements — what to switch off while testing

**[D] + [C]** — Enhancements (auto-crop, music, text generation, image expansion, catalogue overlays) are on by default. During a *learning* test they are poison: Meta silently alters your creative, so you no longer know which variable produced the result.

| Enhancement | Testing phase | Scaling phase |
|---|---|---|
| Text/headline generation | **OFF** — it will rewrite your carefully-bounded claims and can create policy risk | Optional |
| Image expansion / auto-crop | **OFF** — it will crop text off your static ads | ON |
| Music, 3D animation, visual touch-ups | **OFF** | Test it |
| Advantage+ placements | **ON** — you cannot afford manual placement fragmentation | ON |

**This is the single highest-value setting on the whole list for you.** An auto-generated headline on a religious-claims ad is exactly how a small account gets a policy strike.

# PHASE 4 — BUYER PSYCHOLOGY

## 4.1 The five questions that matter

**What stops the scroll?**
A **spatial contradiction**, not a claim. The brain stops for a mismatch it can see: a map with the wrong thing on it, two mountains side by side, an arrow pointing in an unexpected direction. Text claims get scrolled past; a map that looks *wrong* gets a half-second of processing, and half a second is the whole game. **[D]**

**What produces "Wait… what?"**
Not "Mount Sinai was in Africa" — that reads as an assertion, and assertions trigger defence. The reliable trigger is the **honest gap**: *"Nobody has ever found Mount Sinai."* That's verifiable, surprising to most people, and it opens a hole the reader needs filled. Then: *"So how did we all agree where it was?"* **[B]** curiosity-gap mechanics; **[A-verified]** the underlying fact.

**What makes them click?**
The sense that an *answer exists and is one tap away*. Curiosity without a promise of resolution just creates irritation.

**What makes them distrust?**
Overclaiming, exclamation marks, "they don't want you to know", stock-photo Jesus, cheap typography, and any hint that the book is selling identity rather than investigating a question. **This audience's scam-detector is well trained.**

**What makes them buy rather than just Google it?**
Google gives fragments. A book promises the **assembled case** — sequence, sources, maps, counter-arguments answered. Your job in the ad is to signal *structure*, not information. "173 pages, 40 maps, 6 objections answered" outsells "learn the truth". **[D]**

## 4.2 Sixteen psychological angles

# PHASE 5 — POSITIONING STRATEGIES

Seven positions. **Not ranked** — each has a different failure mode and needs different evidence to validate.

---

### P1 — "The Mountain Nobody Has Found"

- **Core promise:** The location you were taught is a tradition, not a discovery — and the search is still open.
- **Audience:** B, D, E
- **Emotional trigger:** Destabilised certainty, then curiosity
- **Reason to believe:** No archaeological consensus; Byzantine-era origin of the traditional site — both verifiable independently of the book
- **Strongest hook:** *"Nobody has ever found Mount Sinai. So how did we all agree where it was?"*
- **Objection:** "Absence of proof isn't evidence for your alternative." **True — the ad must open the question, the book must close it.**
- **Ad concept:** Static. Traditional-site photograph, half-faded, one line of type.
- **Landing page:** Opens on the gap; the African case is the *answer*, revealed mid-page.
- **Risk:** Sounds like it attacks tradition. Mitigate: respect the tradition explicitly.
- **Test:** Does the pure-gap hook beat an Africa-forward hook on CTR *and* on landing-page scroll depth?

---

### P2 — "The Continent in the Margin"

- **Core promise:** Africa may be central to the Exodus rather than adjacent to it.
- **Audience:** A (primary), D
- **Emotional trigger:** Belonging, restoration
- **Reason to believe:** Egypt is in Africa; the Sinai Peninsula straddles the boundary; the book's case
- **Strongest hook:** *"The Exodus begins in Africa. The question is where it ends."*
- **Objection:** "Is this scholarship or identity marketing?"

# PHASE 10 — OFFER ECONOMICS

## 10.1 Market price anchors (verified)

| Format | Typical US price | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nonfiction ebook | **$4.99–$9.99** ("shorter guide/memoir $2.99–4.99; comprehensive $7.99–9.99") | [ManuscriptReport](https://manuscriptreport.com/blog/book-pricing-strategy) |
| KDP 70% royalty band | **$2.99–$12.99** (ceiling raised from $9.99, effective 7 Jul 2026) | [Built&Written](https://www.builtwritten.com/blog/amazon-changed-book-pricing-rules-2026), [KDP](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200634560) |
| KDP paperback royalty | 60% of list minus print cost above $9.99; e.g. **$16.99 list, 200pp → ~$6.79** | [ManuscriptReport royalty calculator](https://manuscriptreport.com/tools/royalty-calculator) |

**The 2026 ceiling rise to $12.99 matters to you.** It means a premium-priced ebook keeps the 70% rate — and as the next section shows, price is the variable that decides whether this campaign can work at all.

## 10.2 The formulas

```
CPC              = CPM ÷ 1000 ÷ CTR
Cost per LPV     = CPC ÷ (LPV ÷ clicks)          [click→LPV leakage, assume 0.85]
CM (contribution)= Price − COGS − payment fees − shipping − refund allowance
Break-even CPA   = CM
Max allowable CAC= CM × (1 − target profit share)
CPA              = Cost per LPV ÷ CVR
Required CVR     = Cost per LPV ÷ CM
Break-even CPC   = CM × CVR × 0.85
Break-even ROAS  = 1 ÷ (CM ÷ Price)
```

## 10.3 The traffic-cost baseline

Using 2026 all-industry US benchmarks — **CPM $11.54**, **Books & Music CTR 2.34%** ([Influee](https://influee.co/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks), [Lebesgue](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/facebook-benchmarks-by-industry-ctr-cpm-cr-and-cac)):

```
CPC          = 11.54 ÷ 1000 ÷ 0.0234 = $0.493
Cost per LPV = 0.493 ÷ 0.85          = $0.580
```

**[D] Treat $0.49 CPC / $0.58 per landing-page view as your planning assumption, and expect it to be optimistic.** A new account with no conversion history, in a niche, from a non-US billing country, will likely see higher CPMs at first. Plan at **$0.70–0.90 per landing-page view** until your own data says otherwise.

## 10.4 Scenario table — this is the decisive analysis

| # | Offer | Price | CM after fees/refunds | Break-even CPA | **Required LP CVR** | Verdict at $0.58/LPV |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ebook, own site | $9.99 | ~$9.00 | $9.00 | **6.4%** | ❌ Above the 2.5–5% cold-LP range. Does not close. |
| 2 | Ebook, KDP 70% | $9.99 | ~$6.90 | $6.90 | **8.4%** | ❌ And you can't even track it. |
| 3 | Paperback, KDP | $16.99 | ~$6.79 | $6.79 | **8.5%** | ❌ No. |
| 4 | Ebook premium | $19.99 | ~$18.80 | $18.80 | **3.1%** | ⚠️ Plausible — top of the normal range |
| 5 | **Bundle: ebook + maps + audio** | **$29** | **~$27.20** | **$27.20** | **2.1%** | ✅ **Comfortably inside the normal range** |
| 6 | Bundle + paperback (print-on-demand, US-fulfilled) | $39 | ~$24.00 | $24.00 | **2.4%** | ✅ Works, more moving parts |
| 7 | Free chapter → email → $29 bundle | — | ~$27.20 at 20% email conversion | Effective CPA ÷ 0.20 | Lead CVR **~10%**, then email does the selling | ✅ **Lowest required page performance** |

### The conclusion you should not skip

**[D] A $9.99 standalone ebook sold cold to US traffic on Meta cannot be made profitable on the first purchase at current CPMs.** The required conversion rate exceeds what good landing pages achieve. This is not a creative problem or a targeting problem — it is arithmetic, and no amount of testing will fix it.

**You have three ways out, and you should use two of them:**

1. **Raise AOV to ~$25–35** with a bundle the book can genuinely support: ebook + high-resolution map pack + audio reading + a sources/further-reading appendix. This moves required CVR from 6.4% to ~2.1% — from impossible to ordinary.
2. **Sell the chapter, not the book** (scenario 7). A free-chapter email capture converts at ~10% instead of ~2%, meaning your ad budget buys **five times as many relationships**. The email sequence then sells the bundle over days, for free. **At KES 1,000–10,000 this is the only funnel with a realistic chance of producing a sale.**
3. Accept that ads are a **brand/audience-building** cost and monetise through Amazon organic ranking — real, but unmeasurable, and I don't recommend betting a tiny budget on it.

**Recommended offer [D]:** `Free chapter (email) → $29 bundle, with a $19.99 ebook-only option at the bottom of the page.` The decoy structure makes the bundle look like the sensible choice.

## 11.3 The thank-you-page trick — most important technical item here

Most lightweight checkout platforms (Payhip, Gumroad, Lemon Squeezy) let you set a **post-purchase redirect URL**. Point it at a page on *your* domain:

```
yourdomain.com/thank-you?src=purchase
```

Fire `Purchase` with value and currency on that page. This gives you reliable purchase tracking **regardless of whether your checkout platform integrates with Meta at all** — which is the usual failure point for sellers using non-Shopify tooling.

**⚠️ Verify before you commit to a platform:** (a) does it support a custom redirect URL, (b) does it let you inject a pixel, (c) does it pay out to Kenya. Check all three *before* building the funnel — this is the single most likely place for the whole plan to hit a wall. Payouts to Kenya are a real constraint: Gumroad raised its payout minimum to $100 in March 2026, Payhip pays out instantly but its merchant-of-record coverage is EU/UK only, and Lemon Squeezy is a merchant of record paying out via PayPal in 200+ countries ([comparison](https://www.wearefounders.uk/best-platforms-for-selling-digital-products-in-2026/), [Payhip](https://payhip.com/blog/gumroad-alternatives/)). **Confirm current terms directly with each platform — these change often.**

**Guard against inflation:** the thank-you page will occasionally be loaded by someone sharing the link. At your volume you'll be reconciling manually against your checkout dashboard anyway, so this is a non-issue now — just don't trust the pixel number over the payout number.

## 13.2 What sample size actually costs — the number nobody tells you

To distinguish a 1.5% CTR from a 2.5% CTR at 80% power and 95% confidence, you need roughly **3,000 impressions per ad**:

```
n ≈ [z₀.₉₇₅·√(2p̄q̄) + z₀.₈₀·√(p₁q₁+p₂q₂)]² ÷ (p₁−p₂)²
  ≈ [1.96(0.198) + 0.84(0.198)]² ÷ (0.01)²
  ≈ 0.307 ÷ 0.0001  ≈  3,070 impressions per ad
```

|---|---|---|---|
| 2 creatives, CTR, big difference (1.5% vs 2.5%) | ~6,200 impressions | ~$72 | **~KES 10,800** |

**Read that last row again.** A statistically sound purchase-rate A/B test costs about **KES 690,000**. This is why Phase 14 tests cheap upstream metrics and *infers* downstream — and why anyone promising you conversion insights from KES 1,000 is lying.

**Verdict: this cannot test purchase conversion. It cannot reliably test creative either.** ~577 impressions split three ways is ~190 impressions per ad. 3. **Then** spend the KES 1,000 on the smoke test above, using the organic winner.
4. **Accumulate to KES 10,000 before running a real test.**

*You asked me to challenge your assumptions. The assumption worth challenging is that KES 1,000 should go into the auction at all. Its highest-value use is operational validation, not learning.*

# PHASE 14 — TESTING FRAMEWORK

## 14.1 The rule you must not break

> **Change one variable per test. The variable you change is the only thing you may draw a conclusion about.**

If you run Creative A → Audience A against Creative B → Audience B, a difference in results is **uninterpretable**. You have two explanations and no way to separate them. This is the most common way small advertisers waste money — not by spending too little, but by spending it on tests that can't produce an answer.

**One legitimate exception [D]:** at the very beginning, you may test *whole concepts* (creative + copy + headline moving together as one "idea"). You're not isolating a variable — you're screening ideas. **Just be honest in your notes that you tested a concept, not a hook.**

## 14.2 The testing hierarchy

| Level | Question | Metric | Cost to answer | Failure means |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L1** | Does it get attention? | CPM, 3s video views, impressions-to-engagement | Cheapest | The idea is invisible. Change the *visual* |
| **L2** | Does it generate clicks? | **CTR (link)**, CPC | Cheap | The hook interests nobody. Change the *hook* |
| **L3** | Do they engage with the page? | **LPV rate** (LPV ÷ clicks), scroll depth, time on page | Moderate | Ad/page mismatch, or the page is slow. See 14.5 |
| **L4** | Do they signal intent? | **Lead rate** (free chapter), AddToCart, InitiateCheckout | Expensive | The offer isn't compelling, or the page doesn't persuade |
| **L5** | Do they buy? | **Purchase CVR, CPA, ROAS** | Very expensive | Price, trust, or checkout friction |

**[D] Spend your money on L1–L3 and infer L4–L5.** Reversing this is what bankrupts small advertisers: they optimise for purchases they can't afford to measure, while never learning whether their hook even works.

## 14.3 The testing matrix — order and reasoning

| Order | Variable | Why here | Budget needed | Test design |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Hook / core idea** | Biggest effect size by far. A hook difference moves CTR 2–5×; a CTA difference moves it 5% | KES 10,000+ | 1 ad set, 2–3 ads, identical everything except image + on-image headline |
| **2** | **Format** (static vs video) | Second-largest effect; also determines your production plan | KES 10,000+ | Same hook, two formats |
| **3** | **Offer** (free chapter vs direct buy) | Changes the economics more than any creative variable (Phase 10) | KES 15,000+ | Same ad, two landing pages |
| **4** | **Landing page structure** | Only testable once traffic is consistent | KES 20,000+ | Same ad, page A vs page B |
| **5** | **Primary text length** | Real but modest | KES 15,000+ | Short vs long body |
| **6** | **Audience** | **Deliberately last** — because Advantage+ makes audience the least controllable variable now. Testing it early wastes money on a lever you barely hold | KES 25,000+ | Broad vs interest-seeded, same creative |
| **7** | **CTA button** | Smallest effect of all | Any | Fold into a later test |

**Why hook first, not audience [D]:** in 2020 audience was the highest-leverage lever. In 2026 it isn't — detailed targeting is a suggestion, exclusions are gone, and Advantage+ overrides your selections anyway. **Creative is now the targeting.** Meta finds the audience *for* your creative; your job is to make creative that a findable audience responds to. Testing audiences first in 2026 is fighting the last war.

## 14.4 Test design template

```
TEST ID:        T-001
HYPOTHESIS:     "Inherited assumption" (S3) will out-CTR "unfound mountain" (S1)
                because self-recognition beats mystery on cold traffic.
VARIABLE:       On-image headline + image. ALL ELSE IDENTICAL.
HELD CONSTANT:  audience, placement, objective, body copy, headline field,
                CTA button, landing page, budget, schedule, ratio
SUCCESS METRIC: Link CTR
MIN SAMPLE:     3,000 impressions per ad
STOP CONDITION: both ads ≥3,000 impressions, OR budget exhausted
DECISION RULE:  winner = higher CTR AND difference ≥ 0.8 percentage points.
                Below that gap → INCONCLUSIVE. Say so. Do not pick a winner.
RESULT:         ___________
CONFIDENCE:     ___________
NEXT ACTION:    ___________
```

**The "INCONCLUSIVE" line is the most important one in this document.** Most small advertisers never write it down, so every test produces a "winner", and they spend months building on noise.

## 14.5 Reading the funnel

Log this table for every test:

| Stage | Metric | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | CPM | spend ÷ impressions × 1000 |
| Attention | CTR | link clicks ÷ impressions |
| Cost | CPC | spend ÷ link clicks |
| **Leakage** | **LPV rate** | **landing page views ÷ link clicks** |
| Engagement | Scroll 25% | ViewContent ÷ LPV |
| Intent | Lead rate | leads ÷ LPV |
| Purchase | CVR | purchases ÷ LPV |

**LPV rate is the most under-watched number in small-budget advertising.** If it's below ~80%, people are clicking and leaving before the page renders — almost always page speed. That's a free fix worth more than any creative test: compress images, drop web fonts, target <2s load on 3G.

# PHASE 15 — METRICS AND DIAGNOSTICS

## 15.1 Definitions and what each one actually tells you

| Metric | Definition | What it really measures | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CPM** | Cost per 1,000 impressions | Auction competitiveness + your relevance | Rising CPM on a stable ad = audience fatigue or seasonal competition |
| **CTR (link)** | Link clicks ÷ impressions | **Hook strength.** Your L2 metric | Use *link* CTR, not "all CTR" — the latter counts likes and expansions and flatters you |
| **CPC** | Spend ÷ link clicks | Cost of one interested human | A function of CPM *and* CTR — always diagnose which one moved |
| **LPV rate** | LPVs ÷ link clicks | **Technical leakage.** <80% = page speed | Usually the cheapest fix available |
| **ViewContent** | 25% scroll (as you've defined it) | Whether the page earned a read | Define it once and never change it mid-test |
| **AddToCart / InitiateCheckout** | Standard events | Offer appeal and checkout friction | Big IC→Purchase gap = checkout problem, not ad problem |
| **Purchase CVR** | Purchases ÷ LPV | Page + offer + trust combined | Needs hundreds of conversions to compare reliably |
| **CPA** | Spend ÷ purchases | The number that decides profitability | Compare to **CM**, never to a benchmark |
| **ROAS** | Revenue ÷ spend | Efficiency | Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin. For a ~93% margin digital bundle that's **~1.08** |
| **AOV** | Revenue ÷ orders | Your most controllable economic lever (Phase 10) | Raising AOV beats optimising ads |
| **CAC** | Total acquisition cost ÷ new customers | CPA plus everything else | At your scale ≈ CPA |
| **Frequency** | Impressions ÷ reach | Audience exhaustion | >2.5 on cold traffic in a short window = pool too small or budget too concentrated |

## 15.2 Reference ranges — use with suspicion

2026 figures, all-industry US: **CPM $11.54 · CTR 1.49% · CPC $0.77**; range CPM $5.61 (apparel) → $28.40 (finance), CTR 0.56% (tech) → 2.24% (fitness). Books & Music CTR ~**2.34%**; Media & Publishing ROAS ~**1.17** — [Influee](https://influee.co/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks), [Lebesgue](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/facebook-benchmarks-by-industry-ctr-cpm-cr-and-cac), [ConversionStudio](https://conversion.studio/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks). Cold-traffic LP CVR: dedicated pages **2.5–5%**, product pages **1.5–3.5%** — [ClickMinded](https://www.clickminded.com/landing-page-conversion-benchmark/).

**[D] Three warnings.** (1) These are aggregates across advertisers with budgets thousands of times yours — your variance will be far wider. (2) That Media & Publishing ROAS of 1.17 is *barely above break-even for a high-margin product and below it for a low-margin one* — it corroborates Phase 10's conclusion that book advertising is structurally thin. (3) **Your own first campaign is a better benchmark than any of this.** After Plan C you'll have real numbers; use those.

## 15.3 Diagnostic decision tree

| Symptom | Most likely cause | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| **High CPM + low CTR** | Creative is invisible *and* irrelevant — Meta penalises low engagement with higher costs | Change the **visual**, not the copy. L1 failure |
| **Low CTR + good LP conversion** | Hook too narrow — it's filtering to a tiny, high-quality slice | Keep the page. Write broader hooks against the same promise |
| **High CTR + low purchase rate** | **Curiosity-bait mismatch** — the ad promised a different thing than the page delivers | Check hook→headline continuity. Or the click was entertainment, not intent |
| **Low CPC + high bounce** | Cheap, unqualified clicks. Often a too-broad or too-vague hook | Add a qualifier to the hook: "a 240-page investigation" filters out casual clickers |
| **Good CTR + good IC + poor purchase completion** | **Checkout friction.** Not an ads problem at all | Test checkout yourself on a US mobile connection. Payment method availability, currency confusion, forced account creation |
| **Good everything, still unprofitable** | **Price/offer economics** (Phase 10) | Raise AOV. Do not try to out-optimise arithmetic |
| **CPM rising, CTR falling over time** | Creative fatigue | Frequency >2.5? Refresh creative, don't raise budget |
| **Strong day 1, collapses day 3** | Learning phase instability, or you edited the ad set | Never edit a live ad set mid-test |
| **Impressions but almost no clicks** | Wrong placement mix, or text unreadable at mobile size | Shrink your ad to thumbnail and look at it |
| **Zero delivery** | Policy review, low bid at tiny budget, or audience too narrow | Check Account Quality first |

## 15.4 Metrics to ignore

**[D]** Reactions, page likes, "engagement rate", shares, "estimated ad recall lift", and Meta's Quality/Engagement/Conversion rankings when you have under 500 impressions. At your budget these are noise dressed as insight. The only numbers that should influence a decision are: **CPM, link CTR, CPC, LPV rate, lead rate, CPA.**

# PHASE 16 — DECISION RULES

## 16.1 The principle

**Never make a decision based on spend alone.** "Kill after $5" is arbitrary — $5 buys 8 impressions in one auction and 800 in another. **Decide on sample size relative to what you're measuring, and on economics relative to your contribution margin.**

## 16.2 Minimum data before *any* decision

| To judge… | Minimum before you may act |
|---|---|
| CPM | 1,000 impressions |
| CTR (directional) | 1,000 impressions per ad |
| CTR (defensible) | **3,000 impressions per ad** |
| LPV rate | 50 clicks |
| Lead rate | 100 LPVs **and** ≥5 leads |
| Purchase rate | 500 LPVs **and** ≥10 purchases |
| CPA (for scaling) | spend ≥ **3× target CPA** with ≥3 conversions |

**Below these thresholds the correct decision is always: keep running or stop for budget reasons — never "this creative lost".**

## 16.3 The five decisions

### 🟢 SCALE
All of:
- Spend ≥ 3× target CPA **and** ≥3 conversions
- CPA ≤ **50% of CM** (Phase 10)
- Frequency < 2.0
- Performance stable ≥3 days

**Action:** +20% daily budget, then wait 3–4 days **[B]**. Larger jumps reset learning. Duplicating into a new ad set restarts learning from zero — avoid at small budgets.

### 🔵 KEEP
- Spend ≥ 1× target CPA, CPA between **50% and 100% of CM**
- Or: CTR ≥ 1.5× the ad-set average with adequate impressions

**Action:** leave it alone. Do not edit a working ad.

### 🟡 ITERATE
- CTR strong (≥1.5× average) **but** CPA > CM → attention works, conversion doesn't
- Or: CTR weak **but** LP conversion strong → conversion works, attention doesn't

**Action:** keep the working half, change the broken half. See Phase 17.

### 🟠 PAUSE
- Spend ≥ 2× target CPA with **zero** conversions **and** below-average CTR
- Or: frequency > 3.0 with declining CTR (fatigue, not failure)
- Or: CPM > 2× the ad-set average with no CTR advantage

**Action:** pause, don't delete. Keep the data and the social proof on the post. Fatigued ads often work again after a rest.

### 🔴 KILL
All of:
- Spend ≥ **3× target CPA**, zero conversions
- ≥3,000 impressions
- CTR < 50% of the ad-set average
- No diagnosable technical fault (page loads, pixel fires, link works)

**Action:** archive, and **write down why**. A kill log is how you stop re-testing the same dead idea in six months.

## 16.4 How to avoid killing an ad too early

Five safeguards:

1. **Check the impression count before you look at the CTR.** Under 1,000 impressions, the CTR field is decoration.
2. **Compute the interval.** At 500 impressions, a measured 2.0% CTR has a 95% interval of roughly **0.8%–3.2%**. That interval contains both "great" and "terrible". You do not have an answer.
3. **Never judge on day one.** Delivery is unstable during learning **[B]**.
4. **Diagnose before you kill.** Zero conversions with a broken pixel is a tracking bug, not a creative failure. Click your own ad and complete a purchase before killing anything.
5. **Decide the stop condition before launch** (the template in 14.4). Rules written in advance protect you from the urge to intervene on day two.

**[D] The opposite failure is real too.** Do not keep an ad alive at 5× target CPA because "it just needs more data". The threshold table in 16.2 cuts both ways.

# PHASE 17 — CREATIVE ITERATION

## 17.1 The diagnostic principle

Every ad does two jobs: **earn the click** (hook, visual, headline) and **earn the purchase** (promise, page, offer). When results are lopsided, **one job is working and one isn't. Change only the broken one.**

## 17.2 The scenarios you asked about

### High CTR + poor sales
**Diagnosis:** the hook is writing a cheque the page can't cash — or it attracted curiosity rather than intent.

**Fix in this order** (cheapest first):
1. **Message-match.** Does the LP H1 echo the ad's exact words? If the ad says "Nobody has ever found Mount Sinai" and the page opens "Discover the truth about biblical geography", you've broken continuity and lost them in three seconds. **Fix this first — it's free and it's the cause most of the time.**
2. **Qualify the hook.** Add a price/format/length signal. "A 240-page investigation, $29" costs you clicks and gains you buyers.
3. **Offer.** Swap direct-buy for the free chapter (Phase 10, scenario 7).
4. **Price.** Only after 1–3.
- **Do not change the creative.** It's doing its job.

### Lower CTR + much higher purchase rate
**Diagnosis:** the ad is **self-qualifying** — filtering to genuine buyers. This is usually a *good* ad that looks bad on the dashboard.

**Action:**
1. **Judge it on CPA, not CTR.** If CPA beats the high-CTR ad, it is the better ad, full stop.
2. Keep the promise; widen the *visual* appeal only. Better photography, stronger typography — same words.
3. **This is your scaling candidate.** High-CTR/low-CVR ads break when you scale them; self-qualifying ads hold.
4. **[D] It's also telling you something about your positioning:** a restrained ad outperforming a curiosity ad means your buyer is the serious reader, not the casual scroller. That should change your whole media plan.

### One image works across multiple audiences
**Diagnosis:** you've found a **universal visual mechanism**, not an audience-specific one — and Advantage+ is probably finding the same people regardless of your audience settings anyway.

**Action:** make it your control. Stop testing audiences (you're not really controlling them). Test *hooks against that image* instead. **[D] This is the most valuable result you can get at a small budget** — one proven visual lets you iterate copy cheaply forever.

### Multiple creatives work but one audience doesn't
**Diagnosis:** two possibilities, and you must separate them.
1. **The audience genuinely doesn't want this** → drop it.
2. **The audience is too small to deliver**, so Meta spent your budget inefficiently → check reach, frequency and CPM. High CPM + low reach = delivery problem, not demand problem.

**Action:** check delivery first. If reach was adequate and results were still poor, drop it and reallocate. Note it in the kill log with the reason.

## 17.3 Iteration ladder — cheapest change first

| Step | Change | Cost | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | LP headline to match ad hook | Free | **Often large** |
| 2 | On-image headline wording | Minutes | Large |
| 3 | Primary text first line | Minutes | Medium |
| 4 | CTA button | Seconds | Small |
| 5 | Image crop / ratio | Minutes | Medium |
| 6 | New image, same idea | Hours | Large |
| 7 | New format (static→video) | Day | Large |
| 8 | New offer/price | Day | **Largest** |
| 9 | New audience | Minutes | Small in 2026 |

**[D] Always work top-down.** Most advertisers jump to step 9 because it's easy to click, when step 1 was the actual problem.

## 17.4 The iteration log

```
DATE | AD ID | CHANGE MADE | CHANGED FROM→TO | WHY | IMPRESSIONS | RESULT | KEEP?
```

**Maintain this religiously.** At your budget, your accumulated documented knowledge is worth more than your ad spend. In six months this log — not your Ads Manager — is the asset.

# PHASE 18 — COMMENTS AND SOCIAL PROOF

## 18.1 Why comments matter more for this product than almost any other

You are advertising a contested historical claim to an audience that contains genuine experts. **Your comment section will be adversarial, and it is public — it appears under the ad for every future viewer.** Social proof on Meta ads is largely *comment proof*: a thoughtful thread reads as credibility; an unanswered hostile thread reads as defeat.

**[D] Comments are also a free research channel.** The objections raised in the first week will be the same objections in your reviews. Harvest them into your landing-page FAQ.

## 18.2 Non-negotiable rules

1. **Never fabricate a comment, review, testimonial or endorsement.** Not with a second account, not with friends posing as readers, not "just to seed the thread."
2. **Never delete a substantive critical comment.** Hide only spam, slurs and off-topic abuse. Deleting real criticism gets screenshotted.
3. **Never argue theology in the comments.** You're selling a geographical investigation. Stay there.
4. **Never claim credentials, endorsements or reviews you don't have.**

## 18.3 Pinned comment (post this within minutes of every ad going live)

> **A note from the author:** This book argues an unconventional position, and it doesn't pretend the question is settled. The location of Mount Sinai has never been established archaeologically, and several sites are seriously defended in the literature. *Sinai in Africa* makes a case for another — with the sources laid out so you can check them.
>
> If you think the argument is wrong, chapter [N] engages the strongest objections directly. I'd rather you disagreed after reading it than agreed without.
>
> Read the opening chapter free: [LINK]

**Why this works [D]:** it pre-empts the top objection, sets a civil tone, signals confidence, and gives every later reader the framing before they read the critics. It is also a second CTA at zero cost.

## 18.4 Response playbook

| Comment type | Example | Response template |
|---|---|---|
| **"Where's your evidence?"** | Fair, common | "Reasonable question. The case rests on [2–3 specific categories — CONFIRM FROM BOOK], with sources listed at the back. Chapter one is free here: [LINK] — it's the fastest way to judge whether the argument holds." |
| **Informed scholarly objection** | Cites Hoffmeier, ABR, etc. | "That's a serious objection and the book addresses it in chapter [N]. Short version: [1 sentence]. I'd genuinely value your read on whether the response holds up." |
| **"This is nonsense"** | No argument offered | "Could be. What's the strongest reason you'd give against it? Happy to point you to where the book takes it on." *(Answer once. Then stop.)* |
| **"Mount Sinai is in Arabia"** | Rival hypothesis | "That's the most-argued alternative — Blum, Cornuke and Richardson all make that case. The book engages it directly in chapter [N] and explains why it goes a different way." |
| **Theological accusation** | "This is heresy" | "The book is about geography and history — where an event happened, not whether the text is trustworthy. Readers of many convictions have found the question worth examining." *(Once. Then disengage.)* |
| **Racial/identity flame** | From any direction | **Do not engage.** Hide if abusive. Never respond to bait about the African angle. |
| **Genuine curiosity** | "What's the main argument?" | "Three strands: [CONFIRM FROM BOOK]. The opening chapter lays out the first one free: [LINK]" |
| **"Is the author qualified?"** | Fair | "[HONEST ANSWER]. The argument is set out so it can be checked against the sources rather than taken on authority." |

**Response discipline:** reply within 4 hours where you can (comments compound), **answer once and stop**, never reply twice to the same hostile commenter, always stay calmer than the commenter, and never use the words "they don't want you to know".

## 18.5 Operating without testimonials

You have none. That's fine — here's the ladder, in order of what to do now:

1. **Substitute *specificity* for social proof.** "[N] maps · [N] sources cited · [PAGE COUNT] pages · objections addressed in chapter [N]" does the same trust job as a review, and is verifiable.
2. **Substitute *transparency*.** The free chapter is stronger proof than any testimonial: you're inviting them to check.
3. **Substitute *engagement*.** A comment thread where the author answers hard questions well *is* social proof — and it's the kind you can build honestly, starting today.
4. **Build real proof deliberately.** After your first 20 buyers: email them, ask for two honest sentences, ask permission to quote, quote exactly. Then add §10 to the landing page.
5. **Seek genuine editorial review.** Submit to real review outlets and podcasts in the biblical-history space. Slow, free, and durable.

**[D] The fastest honest path to social proof for this book is a strong comment section.** Answer the sceptics well and publicly, and within a few weeks you'll have something better than testimonials: a visible record of the author engaging criticism without flinching.

# PHASE 19 — TRUST AND CONTROVERSY

## 19.1 The strategic case for restraint

Aggressive framing ("THEY LIED TO YOU", "THE TRUTH THEY HID") will get you a higher CTR. It will also, in this specific category, produce: policy strikes on a new account you cannot afford to lose, an audience of conspiracy-adjacent readers with low book-purchase rates and high refund rates, hostile scholarly attention that defines the book's reputation before it has one, and one-star reviews from readers who expected revelations and got geography.

**[D] Restraint here is not an ethical tax on performance. It is the performance strategy** — because your buyer is a person who reads 240-page nonfiction about biblical geography, and that person is repelled by hype. You are not selling to the audience that clicks conspiracy bait. You are selling to the audience that clicks *past* it.

## 19.2 The substitution table

| ❌ Never | ✅ Instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Scientists have proven Mount Sinai is in Africa" | "There is no archaeological consensus on where Mount Sinai stood. This book argues for a location in Africa." | First is false and a misinformation-policy risk |
| "The truth they don't want you to know" | "A question that has never been settled" | Conspiracy framing without demonstrable suppression |
| "Everyone has been lied to" | "An assumption most of us absorbed without examining" | No villain, same destabilising effect |
| "The Bible proves Sinai is in Africa" | "The author argues the text's geographic detail points elsewhere" | Attributes the interpretation to the author |
| "Historians are hiding this" | "This position is a minority one, and the book explains why" | Honest, and more interesting |
| "Only 24 hours left!" (when untrue) | "[Real offer, real deadline]" or no deadline | Fake urgency is a policy violation and destroys trust |
| "As a believer, you need to know…" | "Readers of biblical history have been asking…" | **Second-person religious assertion violates Meta's personal-attributes policy** |
| "African Americans deserve to know their history" | "The African context of these narratives is rarely examined closely" | Same reason — ethnic attribution to the viewer |
| "Shocking discovery!" | "An unconventional argument, laid out in full" | Overclaim invites scrutiny you can't survive |

## 19.3 The tone test

Before any ad ships, read it aloud and ask: **"Would a careful historian who disagrees with this book find this ad objectionable?"**

If yes → rewrite. If they'd find it *wrong but fair*, you're in the right place. That is the tonal target: **"Come and investigate this,"** never **"Everyone has been lying to you."**

## 19.4 The risk you cannot insure against

**[D]** If this book's thesis is weak, no advertising strategy saves it — it just gets you to a bad outcome faster and more publicly. The best protection available is the one that's also the best marketing: engage the counter-arguments honestly, mark interpretation as interpretation, and let readers judge. That is both the ethical position and, in this category, the commercially optimal one.

# PHASE 20 — META POLICY CHECK

> **⚠️ Verification caveat:** `transparency.meta.com` and `facebook.com` were blocked by this environment's proxy, so I could not read Meta's policy pages directly. What follows is sourced from search summaries of those pages and from specialist secondhand analysis. **Before launch, read the primary sources yourself at `transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/` — this is a 30-minute job that protects your entire account.**

## 20.1 The policy that will actually catch you: Personal Attributes

**The rule:** ads must not contain content that asserts or implies personal attributes — including **religion or beliefs**, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, health, and financial status — [Meta Transparency Center, via search summary](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/); analysis: [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy), [Gripas](https://gripasmarketing.com/meta-ads-misleading-claims-and-personal-attributes-policy-complete-guide-for-2026/).

**The crucial distinction — this is the thing most advertisers get wrong:**
> The policy governs your **ad copy, images and video text** — **not** the audience you select. You may legally target a narrow, relevant audience; what you may not do is write creative that asserts knowledge of the viewer's protected traits.

So: targeting people interested in biblical archaeology = **fine**. Writing "As a Christian, you deserve to know" = **violation**.

| ❌ Violating | ✅ Compliant |
|---|---|
| "As a Christian, you need to read this" | "A book for readers interested in biblical history" |
| "Are you a believer who has wondered…?" | "Many readers of biblical history have wondered…" |
| "African Americans: reclaim your biblical heritage" | "The African context of these narratives, examined closely" |
| "Your faith will never be the same" | "This book examines a question of geography and history" |
| "Fellow Christians know the truth" | "The author argues that…" |

**The mechanical rule [D]: eliminate second-person statements about identity.** Use "many readers", "this book", "the author argues", "people who study X". Apply it to on-image text and video captions too — those are scanned as well. **Enforcement in 2026 has moved toward proactive AI risk assessment, and personal attributes is reported as the most common rejection category** ([Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-policy), [AuditSocials](https://www.auditsocials.com/blog/meta-sensitive-category-ad-targeting-audience-compliance-2026-personal-attributes)).

## 20.2 Other policies in scope

| Policy | Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Misinformation / misleading claims** | **High** if you assert the thesis as proven fact | Attribute every contested claim: "The author argues…", "This book makes the case that…". Never "proven", "scientists confirm", "the truth revealed" |
| **Social Issues, Elections or Politics (SIEP)** | **Low but non-zero.** US social-issue categories include "Civil and social rights", "Education", "Political values and governance" — religion is **not** itself a listed category ([Meta Transparency, via summary](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/SIEP-advertising/SIEP)) | Keep the framing historical/geographical. **Racial-identity framing could draw a "civil and social rights" classification** — another reason P2 must stay evidence-led. If flagged, you'd need advertiser authorisation with a "Paid for by" disclaimer, which requires **US residency/ID** — a real obstacle from Kenya. **Avoid triggering it in the first place.** |
| **Landing page standards** | Medium | Working links, functioning navigation, no popups/interstitials on arrival, accurate representation, real contact info, privacy + refund policies present |
| **Advertising books** | Low | Books are not restricted. **Amazon restricts religion-specific nonfiction ads; Meta does not** — don't confuse the two ([Kathy Ide](https://kathyide.com/amazon-ads-for-religious-books/)) |
| **Sensational / shocking content** | Low-Medium | No "SHOCKING", no fake-news styling, no torn-paper BREAKING banners |
| **Advertiser identity verification** | **Medium-High for you** | Tightened March 2026; ad accounts must be tied to a legitimate business or individual ([AuditSocials](https://www.auditsocials.com/blog/meta-ad-account-legitimacy-verification-requirement-2026), [Adamigo](https://www.adamigo.ai/blog/advertiser-identity-verification-on-meta-ads)). Complete it **before** funding |
| **Special Ad Categories** | Low | Books don't fall under housing/employment/credit/social-issue categories — as long as you don't invite the SIEP classification above |

## 20.3 Pre-flight copy checklist

Run every ad through this before submitting:

- [ ] No second-person statement about the viewer's religion, race, or beliefs
- [ ] Every contested claim attributed ("the author argues", "this book makes the case")
- [ ] No "proven", "scientists confirm", "the truth revealed", "they don't want you to know"
- [ ] No fabricated urgency, scarcity, testimonials or endorsements
- [ ] No fabricated review stars or invented quotes
- [ ] On-image text checked against the same rules as body copy
- [ ] Landing page: working links, privacy policy, refund policy, contact method
- [ ] Landing page claims match ad claims exactly
- [ ] Advantage+ **text generation OFF** (it can invent a non-compliant headline)
- [ ] Nothing that reads as attacking a religious group or defending one against another

## 20.4 If you get rejected

1. Read the stated policy — it names the category.
2. **Fix and resubmit rather than appealing**, at least the first time. Faster, and repeated appeals are a worse signal than an edit.
3. Most likely cause for you, in order: personal attributes (second-person identity language) → misleading claims (unattributed thesis) → landing page (missing policy pages).
4. **Never resubmit the same ad unchanged.** Repeated rejections put a new account at risk.
5. Check Account Quality weekly. **A new Kenyan account advertising a religious-historical claim to the US has a thinner margin for error than an established US ecommerce account. Treat your first ten ads as conservatively as possible.**

# PHASE 21 — ADVERTISING TO THE U.S. FROM KENYA

## 21.1 Direct answers

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Do I need a U.S. Facebook account?** | **No.** |
| **Do I need a U.S. Business Manager?** | **No.** A Kenyan Business Manager can target any country Meta serves. |
| **Can a Kenyan Meta account advertise to U.S. users?** | **Yes.** This is ordinary, supported, and extremely common. |
| **What matters more — advertiser location or audience location?** | **Audience location, by a wide margin.** Your ad set's location setting determines who sees the ad. Your business country affects billing, currency, tax and available payment methods — not reach. |
| **Payment/verification issues?** | **Yes, and these are your real friction.** See 21.2. |
| **Should I create a U.S. entity?** | **Not now.** It's the right move eventually if you scale (US payment processing, Stripe access, lower friction), but it costs money you should be spending on the book. Revisit past ~$1,000/month spend. |
| **Should I use a VPN?** | **No.** Location mismatch between your login, your payment method and your business details is a classic fraud signal and a leading cause of account restriction. It creates the problem it's meant to solve. |
| **Should I create a fake U.S. identity?** | **No.** It violates Meta's terms, it will fail identity verification (tightened March 2026), and a permanent ban would end this project. There is no legitimate version of this and no upside. |

## 21.2 The legitimate lowest-friction setup

1. **Personal Facebook account** — real name, real details, 2FA enabled. If it's brand new, use it normally for a week or two before creating a Business Manager; brand-new accounts that immediately start advertising internationally attract scrutiny.
2. **Business Manager** (business.facebook.com) — real business/author name, real Kenyan address.
3. **Complete identity/business verification immediately** — before funding. **[A]** (secondhand) Since March 2026 accounts must be tied to a legitimate business or individual ([AuditSocials](https://www.auditsocials.com/blog/meta-ad-account-legitimacy-verification-requirement-2026)). Have your national ID and, if you have one, business registration and KRA PIN ready.
4. **Ad account in KES.** **This does not limit who you can advertise to.** It just means you're billed in shillings and see costs in shillings. Your ad account country and currency must match your payment method's country ([Meta Business Help](https://en-gb.facebook.com/business/help/311330675698510)).
5. **Payment: a Kenyan Visa/Mastercard enabled for international online transactions.** M-Pesa is generally not usable for USD-billed accounts ([GetPlu](https://getplu.com/ke/blog/how-to-pay-facebook-ads-kenya)) — with a KES-billed account this is moot. Virtual USD cards are widely used by Kenyan advertisers, but **a card matching your account country is the lowest-risk option**.
6. **Tax settings:** confirm whether you're advertising for business purposes and add a VAT/KRA ID if you have one. This determines whether the 16% VAT is added ([Meta: Kenya VAT](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/187911129790766)). **Worth 16% of your entire budget — do it before your first charge.**
7. **Facebook Page + Instagram account for the book** — real, with a few genuine posts before the first ad. An ad from an empty Page reduces trust and can slow review.
8. **Ad set location: United States.** Consider "People living in this location" rather than "recently in" for cleaner targeting.

## 21.3 The risks that are actually real

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Payment declined by Kenyan bank on international charge | **High** | Call the bank first and enable international online transactions. This is the #1 first-day blocker ([Evertry](https://evertry.co/blog/how-to-pay-for-facebook-instagram-ads-in-kenya-even-if-your-card-keeps-getting-declined/)) |
| 16% VAT eating budget | **High** | Fix the tax setting before funding |
| Account restriction from location/payment mismatch | Medium | Keep login, payment method and business details all consistently Kenyan |
| Policy strike on religious-claims copy | Medium | Phase 20 checklist |
| Higher CPMs than benchmarks (new account, no history) | **High** | Expect it. Plan A exists to measure it |
| Payout friction getting *paid* by US customers | **High** | **Solve this before you advertise** — Phase 11.3. Nothing is worse than ads that work and money you can't collect |

**[D] Point 6 and the last row are the two that most often kill a project like this.** Both are solvable in an afternoon, and both are invisible until they've cost you.

# PHASE 22 — LANDING PAGE PATTERNS FROM BOOK SELLERS

**[C]/[D] Patterns worth extracting** — derived from direct-response book-funnel conventions and the cold-traffic benchmark data cited in Phase 9, not from a live teardown (I couldn't access the pages behind the ads).

| Element | The pattern | Applied to your page |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | Leads with the *question or promise*, not the title | §1 leads with the unfound-mountain fact, not "Sinai in Africa" |
| **Product presentation** | 3D cover render on dark ground; multiple interior spreads | §1 hero + §7 interior gallery — **interior shots matter more than the cover for nonfiction**; they prove substance |
| **Social proof** | Placed immediately after the first CTA | You have none — §10 stays out until it's real (Phase 18.5) |
| **Pricing** | Anchored against a higher-value alternative; bundle as the default choice | $29 bundle as default, $19.99 ebook-only below it (Phase 10) |
| **Excerpt** | Free chapter/sample is near-universal in nonfiction funnels | §8, and it's your **primary** CTA |
| **Author authority** | Mid-page, after the argument | §9 — correct placement for an unknown author |
| **FAQ** | Handles objections as questions rather than defences | §11 + §12 |
| **CTA placement** | Repeated every 1.5 screens on mobile | Hero, §8, §13, plus a sticky mobile bar |
| **Mobile design** | Single column, ≥16px body, thumb-reachable buttons, <2s load | Design at 390px first |
| **Page length** | Long for considered purchases; short for impulse | **Long.** Contested claim + unknown author = maximum evidence burden |
| **Guarantee** | Prominent, unconditional, near the buy button | §14 |

**[D] The pattern most relevant to you:** in nonfiction funnels, the **free sample chapter consistently outperforms a direct-buy button on cold traffic** — because it converts a $29 decision into a $0 decision. Given your budget, this isn't merely a best practice; it's the only structure whose arithmetic works (Phase 10.4, scenario 7).

**Anti-patterns to avoid:** autoplay video with sound, entry popups (a Meta landing-page-standards risk), countdown timers that reset, fake "12 people viewing", stock-photo testimonials, and walls of five-star graphics you haven't earned.
