0.3 Claim discipline for this specific product — non-negotiable

The single biggest way this campaign fails is not bad creative. It is making a claim the book cannot support, getting the ad rejected or the account restricted, and burning a budget you cannot replace.

Category How to phrase it in ads Example
Established fact State plainly "Egypt is in Africa." "The Sinai Peninsula links Africa and Asia."
Long-standing tradition (not proof) Name it as tradition "The traditional site has been honoured since the Byzantine era."
Genuine scholarly uncertainty State as uncertainty "There is no scholarly consensus on where Mount Sinai stood."

PHASE 4 — BUYER PSYCHOLOGY

4.1 The five questions that matter

What stops the scroll? A spatial contradiction, not a claim. The brain stops for a mismatch it can see: a map with the wrong thing on it, two mountains side by side, an arrow pointing in an unexpected direction. Text claims get scrolled past; a map that looks wrong gets a half-second of processing, and half a second is the whole game. D

What produces "Wait… what?" Not "Mount Sinai was in Africa" — that reads as an assertion, and assertions trigger defence. The reliable trigger is the honest gap: "Nobody has ever found Mount Sinai." That's verifiable, surprising to most people, and it opens a hole the reader needs filled. Then: "So how did we all agree where it was?" B curiosity-gap mechanics; [A-verified] the underlying fact.

What makes them click? The sense that an answer exists and is one tap away. Curiosity without a promise of resolution just creates irritation.

What makes them distrust? Overclaiming, exclamation marks, "they don't want you to know", stock-photo Jesus, cheap typography, and any hint that the book is selling identity rather than investigating a question. This audience's scam-detector is well trained.

What makes them buy rather than just Google it? Google gives fragments. A book promises the assembled case — sequence, sources, maps, counter-arguments answered. Your job in the ad is to signal structure, not information. "173 pages, 40 maps, 6 objections answered" outsells "learn the truth". D

4.3 What makes someone share it

People share things that make them look interesting. "Did you know nobody has ever actually found Mount Sinai?" is a dinner-table fact. "Buy my book" is not. Write at least two ads whose hook survives being repeated out loud by someone who hasn't read the book.

PHASE 5 — POSITIONING STRATEGIES

Seven positions. Not ranked — each has a different failure mode and needs different evidence to validate.

P1 — "The Mountain Nobody Has Found"

  • Core promise: The location you were taught is a tradition, not a discovery — and the search is still open.
  • Audience: B, D, E
  • Emotional trigger: Destabilised certainty, then curiosity
  • Reason to believe: No archaeological consensus; Byzantine-era origin of the traditional site — both verifiable independently of the book
  • Strongest hook: "Nobody has ever found Mount Sinai. So how did we all agree where it was?"
  • Objection: "Absence of proof isn't evidence for your alternative." True — the ad must open the question, the book must close it.
  • Ad concept: Static. Traditional-site photograph, half-faded, one line of type.
  • Landing page: Opens on the gap; the African case is the answer, revealed mid-page.
  • Risk: Sounds like it attacks tradition. Mitigate: respect the tradition explicitly.
  • Test: Does the pure-gap hook beat an Africa-forward hook on CTR and on landing-page scroll depth?

P2 — "The Continent in the Margin"

  • Core promise: Africa may be central to the Exodus rather than adjacent to it.
  • Audience: A (primary), D
  • Emotional trigger: Belonging, restoration
  • Reason to believe: Egypt is in Africa; the Sinai Peninsula straddles the boundary; the book's case
  • Strongest hook: "The Exodus begins in Africa. The question is where it ends."
  • Objection: "Is this scholarship or identity marketing?"
  • Ad concept: Continental map, Egypt lit, route ambiguous at the border.
  • Landing page: Evidence first, identity payoff last. Never the reverse.
  • Risk: Highest of all seven. Read as identity-pandering, it loses the audience it targets.
  • Test: Does adding explicit scholarly-restraint language raise or lower conversion in this segment? My prediction: raises it, contrary to normal DR instinct.

P3 — "The Investigation"

  • Core promise: A documented case you can evaluate yourself.
  • Audience: B, D
  • Emotional trigger: Intellectual respect
  • Reason to believe: Maps, primary sources, engagement with counter-arguments
  • Strongest hook: "Four locations. One text. The author makes a case for a fifth."
  • Objection: "Amateur or credentialed?" — requires a real author bio.
  • Ad concept: Evidence-board / document aesthetic.
  • Landing page: Long-form, table of contents, sample chapter, sources visible.
  • Risk: Low CTR. Cold audiences don't click rigour.
  • Test: Does it produce the highest purchase rate per click even at the lowest CTR? If yes, it's your scaling position.

P4 — "Reading the Bible With a Different Map"

  • Core promise: Familiar passages change meaning when the geography moves.
  • Audience: D
  • Emotional trigger: Rediscovery of something loved
  • Reason to believe: Specific verses — must come from your manuscript
  • Strongest hook: "Read Exodus 3 again — this time with a different map open."
  • Objection: "Is this going to unsettle my faith?"
  • Ad concept: Open Bible + map, one verse highlighted.
  • Landing page: Explicit: geography, not doctrine.
  • Risk: Highest policy risk (religion = protected attribute). Keep every line third-person.
  • Test: Does devotional-adjacent framing convert better than investigative framing for audience D?

P5 — "The Assumption You Never Chose"

  • Core promise: You didn't decide this; you inherited it.
  • Audience: All
  • Emotional trigger: Mild indignation without an enemy
  • Reason to believe: The traditional identification does have a traceable historical origin
  • Strongest hook: "You were never taught where Mount Sinai is. You just absorbed it."

§ 2 — THE CURIOSITY SECTION

Most people could point to Mount Sinai on a map. Almost nobody could say why.

The site most of us picture sits in the southern Sinai Peninsula. It has been venerated for well over a thousand years, and the tradition is genuinely ancient and worthy of respect.

But veneration is not identification. The association between that mountain and the Exodus account took hold in the early Byzantine period, as monastic communities spread through the desert. It was never established by excavation — and to this day, no Exodus-period archaeological remains have been recovered along the traditional route.

That is not a fringe position. It is the ordinary state of the evidence, and it is why the question remains genuinely open among historians and archaeologists.

§ 3 — THE CENTRAL QUESTION

What if we have been looking in the wrong direction?

Every major alternative proposed in the last century has moved the mountain east — into Midian, into north-west Arabia, into the Negev.

Sinai in Africa asks a question almost nobody has pressed: what happens if we follow the geography west instead?

The Exodus account begins in Egypt. Egypt is in Africa. The Sinai Peninsula itself sits on the boundary between two continents — placed in south-west Asia by most geographers, and grouped with Africa in most political maps.

The author argues that the African context of the narrative is not incidental background. It is the setting.

§ 6 — WHY IT MATTERS

Geography is not a detail. It is the frame the whole story sits in.

Where an event happened shapes who we picture in it, which cultures we place at its centre, and which parts of the world we treat as the background rather than the setting.

PHASE 14 — TESTING FRAMEWORK

14.1 The rule you must not break

Change one variable per test. The variable you change is the only thing you may draw a conclusion about.

If you run Creative A → Audience A against Creative B → Audience B, a difference in results is uninterpretable. You have two explanations and no way to separate them. This is the most common way small advertisers waste money — not by spending too little, but by spending it on tests that can't produce an answer.

One legitimate exception D: at the very beginning, you may test whole concepts (creative + copy + headline moving together as one "idea"). You're not isolating a variable — you're screening ideas. Just be honest in your notes that you tested a concept, not a hook.

PHASE 16 — DECISION RULES

16.1 The principle

Never make a decision based on spend alone. "Kill after $5" is arbitrary — $5 buys 8 impressions in one auction and 800 in another. Decide on sample size relative to what you're measuring, and on economics relative to your contribution margin.

16.2 Minimum data before any decision

To judge… Minimum before you may act
CPM 1,000 impressions
CTR (directional) 1,000 impressions per ad
CTR (defensible) 3,000 impressions per ad
LPV rate 50 clicks
Lead rate 100 LPVs and ≥5 leads
Purchase rate 500 LPVs and ≥10 purchases
CPA (for scaling) spend ≥ 3× target CPA with ≥3 conversions

Below these thresholds the correct decision is always: keep running or stop for budget reasons — never "this creative lost".

16.3 The five decisions

🟢 SCALE

All of: - Spend ≥ 3× target CPA and ≥3 conversions - CPA ≤ 50% of CM (Phase 10) - Frequency < 2.0 - Performance stable ≥3 days

Action: +20% daily budget, then wait 3–4 days B. Larger jumps reset learning. Duplicating into a new ad set restarts learning from zero — avoid at small budgets.

🔵 KEEP

  • Spend ≥ 1× target CPA, CPA between 50% and 100% of CM
  • Or: CTR ≥ 1.5× the ad-set average with adequate impressions

Action: leave it alone. Do not edit a working ad.

🟡 ITERATE

  • CTR strong (≥1.5× average) but CPA > CM → attention works, conversion doesn't
  • Or: CTR weak but LP conversion strong → conversion works, attention doesn't

Action: keep the working half, change the broken half. See Phase 17.

🟠 PAUSE

  • Spend ≥ 2× target CPA with zero conversions and below-average CTR
  • Or: frequency > 3.0 with declining CTR (fatigue, not failure)
  • Or: CPM > 2× the ad-set average with no CTR advantage

Action: pause, don't delete. Keep the data and the social proof on the post. Fatigued ads often work again after a rest.

🔴 KILL

All of: - Spend ≥ 3× target CPA, zero conversions - ≥3,000 impressions - CTR < 50% of the ad-set average - No diagnosable technical fault (page loads, pixel fires, link works)

Action: archive, and write down why. A kill log is how you stop re-testing the same dead idea in six months.

16.4 How to avoid killing an ad too early

Five safeguards:

  1. Check the impression count before you look at the CTR. Under 1,000 impressions, the CTR field is decoration.
  2. Compute the interval. At 500 impressions, a measured 2.0% CTR has a 95% interval of roughly 0.8%–3.2%. That interval contains both "great" and "terrible". You do not have an answer.
  3. Never judge on day one. Delivery is unstable during learning B.
  4. Diagnose before you kill. Zero conversions with a broken pixel is a tracking bug, not a creative failure. Click your own ad and complete a purchase before killing anything.
  5. Decide the stop condition before launch (the template in 14.4). Rules written in advance protect you from the urge to intervene on day two.

D The opposite failure is real too. Do not keep an ad alive at 5× target CPA because "it just needs more data". The threshold table in 16.2 cuts both ways.

PHASE 17 — CREATIVE ITERATION

17.1 The diagnostic principle

Every ad does two jobs: earn the click (hook, visual, headline) and earn the purchase (promise, page, offer). When results are lopsided, one job is working and one isn't. Change only the broken one.

17.2 The scenarios you asked about

High CTR + poor sales

Diagnosis: the hook is writing a cheque the page can't cash — or it attracted curiosity rather than intent.

Fix in this order (cheapest first): 1. Message-match. Does the LP H1 echo the ad's exact words? If the ad says "Nobody has ever found Mount Sinai" and the page opens "Discover the truth about biblical geography", you've broken continuity and lost them in three seconds. Fix this first — it's free and it's the cause most of the time. 2. Qualify the hook. Add a price/format/length signal. "A 240-page investigation, $29" costs you clicks and gains you buyers. 3. Offer. Swap direct-buy for the free chapter (Phase 10, scenario 7). 4. Price. Only after 1–3. - Do not change the creative. It's doing its job.

Lower CTR + much higher purchase rate

Diagnosis: the ad is self-qualifying — filtering to genuine buyers. This is usually a good ad that looks bad on the dashboard.

Action: 1. Judge it on CPA, not CTR. If CPA beats the high-CTR ad, it is the better ad, full stop. 2. Keep the promise; widen the visual appeal only. Better photography, stronger typography — same words. 3. This is your scaling candidate. High-CTR/low-CVR ads break when you scale them; self-qualifying ads hold. 4. D It's also telling you something about your positioning: a restrained ad outperforming a curiosity ad means your buyer is the serious reader, not the casual scroller. That should change your whole media plan.

One image works across multiple audiences

Diagnosis: you've found a universal visual mechanism, not an audience-specific one — and Advantage+ is probably finding the same people regardless of your audience settings anyway.

Action: make it your control. Stop testing audiences (you're not really controlling them). Test hooks against that image instead. D This is the most valuable result you can get at a small budget — one proven visual lets you iterate copy cheaply forever.

Multiple creatives work but one audience doesn't

Diagnosis: two possibilities, and you must separate them. 1. The audience genuinely doesn't want this → drop it. 2. The audience is too small to deliver, so Meta spent your budget inefficiently → check reach, frequency and CPM. High CPM + low reach = delivery problem, not demand problem.

Action: check delivery first. If reach was adequate and results were still poor, drop it and reallocate. Note it in the kill log with the reason.

PHASE 18 — COMMENTS AND SOCIAL PROOF

18.1 Why comments matter more for this product than almost any other

You are advertising a contested historical claim to an audience that contains genuine experts. Your comment section will be adversarial, and it is public — it appears under the ad for every future viewer. Social proof on Meta ads is largely comment proof: a thoughtful thread reads as credibility; an unanswered hostile thread reads as defeat.

D Comments are also a free research channel. The objections raised in the first week will be the same objections in your reviews. Harvest them into your landing-page FAQ.

18.2 Non-negotiable rules

  1. Never fabricate a comment, review, testimonial or endorsement. Not with a second account, not with friends posing as readers, not "just to seed the thread."
  2. Never delete a substantive critical comment. Hide only spam, slurs and off-topic abuse. Deleting real criticism gets screenshotted.
  3. Never argue theology in the comments. You're selling a geographical investigation. Stay there.
  4. Never claim credentials, endorsements or reviews you don't have.

PHASE 19 — TRUST AND CONTROVERSY

19.1 The strategic case for restraint

Aggressive framing ("THEY LIED TO YOU", "THE TRUTH THEY HID") will get you a higher CTR. It will also, in this specific category, produce: policy strikes on a new account you cannot afford to lose, an audience of conspiracy-adjacent readers with low book-purchase rates and high refund rates, hostile scholarly attention that defines the book's reputation before it has one, and one-star reviews from readers who expected revelations and got geography.

D Restraint here is not an ethical tax on performance. It is the performance strategy — because your buyer is a person who reads 240-page nonfiction about biblical geography, and that person is repelled by hype. You are not selling to the audience that clicks conspiracy bait. You are selling to the audience that clicks past it.

19.2 The substitution table

❌ Never ✅ Instead Why
"Scientists have proven Mount Sinai is in Africa" "There is no archaeological consensus on where Mount Sinai stood. This book argues for a location in Africa." First is false and a misinformation-policy risk
"The truth they don't want you to know" "A question that has never been settled" Conspiracy framing without demonstrable suppression
"Everyone has been lied to" "An assumption most of us absorbed without examining" No villain, same destabilising effect
"The Bible proves Sinai is in Africa" "The author argues the text's geographic detail points elsewhere" Attributes the interpretation to the author
"Historians are hiding this" "This position is a minority one, and the book explains why" Honest, and more interesting
"Only 24 hours left!" (when untrue) "[Real offer, real deadline]" or no deadline Fake urgency is a policy violation and destroys trust

"African Americans deserve to know their history" "The African context of these narratives is rarely examined closely" Same reason — ethnic attribution to the viewer
"Shocking discovery!" "An unconventional argument, laid out in full" Overclaim invites scrutiny you can't survive

19.3 The tone test

Before any ad ships, read it aloud and ask: "Would a careful historian who disagrees with this book find this ad objectionable?"

If yes → rewrite. If they'd find it wrong but fair, you're in the right place. That is the tonal target: "Come and investigate this," never "Everyone has been lying to you."

19.4 The risk you cannot insure against

D If this book's thesis is weak, no advertising strategy saves it — it just gets you to a bad outcome faster and more publicly. The best protection available is the one that's also the best marketing: engage the counter-arguments honestly, mark interpretation as interpretation, and let readers judge. That is both the ethical position and, in this category, the commercially optimal one.

PHASE 20 — META POLICY CHECK

⚠️ Verification caveat: transparency.meta.com and facebook.com were blocked by this environment's proxy, so I could not read Meta's policy pages directly. What follows is sourced from search summaries of those pages and from specialist secondhand analysis. Before launch, read the primary sources yourself at transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/ — this is a 30-minute job that protects your entire account.

20.1 The policy that will actually catch you: Personal Attributes

The rule: ads must not contain content that asserts or implies personal attributes — including religion or beliefs, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, health, and financial status — Meta Transparency Center, via search summary; analysis: Stackmatix, Gripas.

The crucial distinction — this is the thing most advertisers get wrong:

The policy governs your ad copy, images and video text — not the audience you select. You may legally target a narrow, relevant audience; what you may not do is write creative that asserts knowledge of the viewer's protected traits.

So: targeting people interested in biblical archaeology = fine. Writing "As a Christian, you deserve to know" = violation.

❌ Violating ✅ Compliant
"As a Christian, you need to read this" "A book for readers interested in biblical history"
"Are you a believer who has wondered…?" "Many readers of biblical history have wondered…"
"African Americans: reclaim your biblical heritage" "The African context of these narratives, examined closely"
"Your faith will never be the same" "This book examines a question of geography and history"
"Fellow Christians know the truth" "The author argues that…"

The mechanical rule D: eliminate second-person statements about identity. Use "many readers", "this book", "the author argues", "people who study X". Apply it to on-image text and video captions too — those are scanned as well. Enforcement in 2026 has moved toward proactive AI risk assessment, and personal attributes is reported as the most common rejection category (Stackmatix, AuditSocials).

Here is the honest truth, stated plainly: KES 1,000 cannot reliably tell you which creative is better, and it certainly cannot tell you whether anyone will buy the book. ~190 impressions per ad across three ads is statistically meaningless. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something.

So spend it on the one thing it can buy: certainty that your machine works, and your real CPM.

Do these first — all free (Week 1)

Then — free organic screening (Week 2)

Post S1, S3, S5 (and V1, V2 if made) as organic Reels/posts on Facebook, Instagram, TikTok and YouTube Shorts. Watch saves, shares and comments — not likes. This gives you hook data at zero cost, at volumes your ad budget cannot buy.

What you are not buying: a creative winner, an audience answer, or any purchase data. Write that down before you launch, so you're not tempted to over-read the result.

Then
